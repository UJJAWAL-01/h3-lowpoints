    About this map

    Where rain water collects

    For exploration and education only. Not a flood map, forecast, warning or advice. Read the disclaimer.

    Rain that cannot flow away gathers in low ground: a hollow in a field, a dip in a street, a basin between buildings. Elevation models call these closed depressions. This map shows every one on land in the lower 48 states, found by the open-source h3-lowpoints library on the USGS 3DEP elevation model, and drawn on H3 hexagons.

    How to read it

    • Across the country and at city scale, each hexagon says how much of its area lies in depressions.
    • At street scale (the finest level), each hexagon is shaded by how deep water stands there when its depression is full.
    • Columns and Wells show the same numbers in 3D: columns rise, wells sink below the map. Their heights are exaggerated, by a factor you choose and the map states (×1 is true scale, ×10 the default). Drag with the right mouse button, or two fingers, to tilt and turn; the compass button sets north up again.
    • Click a hexagon to see its depression: how much it holds, the rain that fills it, how often that rain falls, and where it overflows.
    • The raindrop opens the rain simulator: choose a storm by how rare it is (NOAA Atlas 14 for each depression's own location) or by how much rain, how long it lasts, and how wet the ground was before. Play shows rain falling from none to the whole storm and the depressions filling and overflowing into one another, with water from neighbouring areas included. The timeline runs in hours: what a depression holds depends only on the rain fallen so far, and NOAA Atlas 14's median pattern for the region says when that rain has fallen (rain reaches each depression as it falls, so times are the earliest possible; where NOAA has no pattern, the timeline is rain fallen). It is computed in 16 exact steps with the same equations as the library; while it plays the map blends smoothly between them, and every number shown is an exact step.

    What it does not include

    • Storm drains, sewers, pipes, culverts and pumps. In cities they carry much of the water away, and many deep depressions are road cuts, underpasses and ramps that drains keep dry; the elevation data cannot show drains. The map flags depressions on major roads, and can hide them.
    • Water soaking into the ground once it is in a depression (the simulator keeps it).
    • Features smaller than the 30 m elevation data can see, such as most street-scale dips.
    • Rivers in flood, tides, snowmelt and frozen ground.

    How it is made

    • Depressions are found in the USGS 3DEP 30 m elevation model of the lower 48 states by filling every pit to the level at which water would spill out (an exact priority-flood fill), keeping those at least 0.5 m deep, with open water masked out.
    • Overflow paths (the blue lines) follow the ground: water from a full depression leaves at the lowest point of its rim and runs downhill, each step to the steepest neighbouring cell, until it reaches the next depression. The map draws them straight between lowest points.
    • The storm that fills each one compares the runoff from its land (SCS curve numbers from Annual NLCD 2024 land cover and USDA gNATSGO soils) with the water it holds, for NOAA Atlas 14 24-hour storms, counting overflow from upstream.
    • Every step is open source and checked; the code, the full dataset and its tests are public. Method · How results are checked

    It is not a flood map or a warning. In an emergency, follow local officials. How well these depressions match real flood reports, including what did not work, is in the evidence.
